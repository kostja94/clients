# How to Create LinkedIn Carousel Posts: The Complete 2026 Guide

LinkedIn carousel posts — formally **document posts** in LinkedIn’s own taxonomy — are the highest-engagement format on the platform, averaging roughly a 24% engagement rate against 7% for plain text posts. They earn more reach, more saves, and more **dwell time** than any other native format; LinkedIn’s mechanics treat every swipe as a micro-interaction and every retained second as a distribution signal. The catch: the gap between knowing carousels outperform and actually producing one weekly is where most operators stall. This guide compares three creation workflows — **manual design**, **single-purpose AI generators**, and **strategy-aware AI agents** — alongside the specs, slide architecture, and posting discipline that separate a deck people swipe through from one they save and forward. For the paid-advertising counterpart, see the companion guide to [LinkedIn carousel ads](https://dynal.ai/blog/linkedin-carousel-ads-complete-guide). LinkedIn’s own help documentation on [sharing documents on LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a542850) covers button-level mechanics; this guide covers editorial and workflow decisions those help pages do not.

The short version: carousels upload as **PDF document posts**; every page renders as a swipeable slide; the sweet spot lives at **8–12 slides** in a **1080×1350 (4:5) portrait ratio**. The three realistic creation paths — manual design, single-purpose generator, or strategy-aware AI agent — matter less than the fundamentals: a **hook** strong enough to earn the first swipe, a **single idea per slide**, and a **clear call to action** on the final frame.

## 1. What is a LinkedIn carousel post?

A LinkedIn carousel — officially called a **document post** — is a multi-page PDF that LinkedIn renders as a swipeable, slide-by-slide visual story directly in the feed. Each PDF page becomes one slide. Viewers swipe through the deck without ever leaving the feed, which dramatically increases **dwell time** and the probability the algorithm surfaces the post to more people.

Held up against the other native formats, the strengths show clearly. Text posts are the fastest to ship but easy to scroll past. Single images stop the eye but offer no second screen of value. Native video buys high dwell time at the price of production work most teams cannot sustain weekly. Carousels sit in the sweet spot — swipe-driven micro-engagement, the highest save rate of any native format, and reusable reference material that keeps surfacing weeks after publishing. For a full breakdown of all LinkedIn post formats and when to use each, see our [types of LinkedIn posts](https://dynal.ai/blog/types-of-linkedin-posts) guide.

The reason the algorithm rewards them is mechanical: every swipe is a small interaction and every second on the post tells LinkedIn the content is worth distributing further.

## 2. Why carousels matter in 2026

The numbers tell a clear story. Multiple 2026 industry benchmarks put carousel engagement at roughly 3.7x text posts, with native document posts pulling about 6x more reach than posts that link out. They also see 3x higher save rates, which matters because saves are a long-tail compounding signal — saved posts keep getting recommended weeks after publishing.

The format does its best work whenever an idea benefits from being broken down rather than crammed into a single paragraph. Step-by-step tutorials map naturally onto one slide per step. Frameworks and mental models translate into visual breakdowns that practically beg to be saved. Listicles ("10 mistakes," "7 templates," "5 ways") build momentum slide by slide. Data roundups land harder when each statistic gets its own card and the takeaway lives on a dedicated final slide. Even case studies and before/after stories gain a narrative arc — setup, obstacle, payoff — that flat text posts struggle to deliver.

## 3. Anatomy of a high-performing carousel

Almost every viral LinkedIn carousel follows the same five-part structure. Internalize this and you can create one in any niche. For the opening line patterns that make slide 1 worth swiping, see our deep dive on [LinkedIn hooks without clickbait](https://dynal.ai/blog/linkedin-hooks-without-clickbait).

**Slide 1 — Hook.** A bold headline that creates curiosity or contradicts a common belief, plus your name or brand mark in the corner. This slide alone decides whether anyone swipes.

**Slide 2 — Promise / context.** Tell the reader exactly what they'll get by swiping through. "Here are five mistakes — and the fix for each."

**Slides 3 to N — Value.** One single idea per slide. Big headline, scannable body, supporting visual. Never crowd two ideas onto one slide.

**Second-to-last — Recap or summary.** A quick recall of the core takeaways so the reader walks away with a memorable structure.

**Last — Call to action.** Ask for one specific action: follow, comment, save, repost, or click to a resource. Make it explicit.

A handful of design rules apply to every slide regardless of topic. LinkedIn’s [document sharing specifications](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a542850) confirm the upload mechanics and file limits referenced throughout this section. Build the deck at 1080×1350 (4:5 portrait) to claim the maximum vertical real estate in the feed. Set body type at 24pt minimum, ideally 32–48pt — most people read carousels on a phone in motion. Limit yourself to a single typeface family, pairing a display font with a clean body font and nothing more. Lean on high contrast (dark on light, or light on dark) and skip fussy gradients behind copy. Finally, drop a page indicator like "1/10" in the same corner of every slide so viewers know the swipe is worth committing to.

## 4. Method 1: Create a carousel manually

The traditional route. You write the copy yourself, design every slide, and export to PDF. Total control, full creative freedom — but it can absorb two to four hours per post.

### 4.1 Step 1: Pick a topic and outline

Start with one focused topic that maps to your audience's pain or curiosity. Use a tested template — listicle, how-to, framework, before/after, mistakes-and-fixes. Outline 7 to 10 core points before opening any design tool. If the topic doesn't fit on a napkin, it won't fit in a carousel.

### 4.2 Step 2: Write slide copy

Limit each slide to 25 words or fewer. One idea per slide, always. Treat the hook slide like a magazine cover line: bold claim, curiosity gap, no jargon. Write all body copy in a separate doc first so you can edit it as one piece before any design happens.

### 4.3 Step 3: Design in Canva or Figma

Set a 1080×1350 px canvas. Build a master template with your brand colors, two fonts, page numbers, and a swipe indicator. Duplicate the master for each slide. The discipline of one template across all slides is what makes a carousel feel premium instead of homemade. If your raw material starts as meeting notes, Slack threads, or PDF exports rather than a blank slide, the ingestion workflow in [turning notes and PDFs into posts](https://dynal.ai/blog/linkedin-posts-from-notes-and-pdfs) gives you a structured capture-to-draft pipeline before design begins.

### 4.4 Step 4: Export as PDF

File → Download → PDF Standard. Confirm the file is under 100MB (LinkedIn's hard limit). Open the PDF and visually inspect each page — once it's posted you cannot edit it.

### 4.5 Step 5: Upload to LinkedIn

Click "Start a post," tap the document icon (the one that looks like a sheet of paper), upload your PDF, give the document a title (this shows above slide 1), write a caption with a strong opening hook, and post.

**Pros:** total brand control, reusable templates, no tool lock-in.

**Cons:** 2–4 hours per post, requires design fluency, hard to do weekly.

## 5. Method 2: Use a single-purpose carousel generator

A second wave of tools emerged specifically to remove the design step. Products like PostNitro, AICarousels, Positiv, Postking, and Carosello all share a similar workflow: you pick a template, paste in a topic or long-form text, and the tool spits out a finished multi-slide PDF you can lightly edit.

The typical generator workflow looks like this:

1. Pick a slide template from the gallery.
2. Enter a topic, or paste a blog post / transcript to be split.
3. The AI splits content across 8–12 slides automatically.
4. Tweak headlines, swap colors, fix typos.
5. One-click PDF export.

Total time: usually 10–15 minutes. The output looks polished out of the box, and most tools have a free tier that's enough to test the workflow.

**Pros:** fast, no design skill required, decent default aesthetics.

**Cons:** templates feel recognizable across users, weak brand identity, monthly subscription, and the tool only does carousels — you still need separate workflows for text posts, scheduling, and analytics.

**Best for:** occasional creators who want to publish a clean carousel now and then without committing to a content system.

## 6. Method 3: Use an AI agent

The newest category. An AI agent doesn't just "generate a PDF" — it owns the whole loop from idea to published post. The mental model is different: instead of a tool you operate, an agent is a teammate you brief.

A typical AI-agent workflow:

1. **Brief.** Give the agent a topic, a URL (a YouTube video, tweet, blog post, or PDF), or a rough idea.
2. **Research.** The agent pulls supporting data, statistics, or examples and cites sources.
3. **Outline.** It generates an 8–12 slide narrative arc — hook, value, recap, CTA — based on your past top-performers.
4. **Copy.** Each slide gets a headline, body, and visual recommendation written in your voice.
5. **Design.** The agent applies your brand palette, fonts, and template across all slides automatically.
6. **Review.** You skim the deck, tweak one or two lines, and approve.
7. **Schedule and post.** Publish immediately or queue it into your content calendar.

The structural difference from a single-purpose generator is that an agent is strategy-aware and cross-format. It can take one core idea and produce a carousel plus a text post plus a short-form video script, all coordinated. It also learns from your previous posts — what hooks performed, what topics drove inbound conversations, what slide structures got the most saves — and applies that to future drafts.

Total time: 5 minutes per carousel, often less, with output quality that improves week over week.

**Pros:** fastest path to publish, scales to weekly or daily cadence, owns content strategy not just design, repurposes across formats.

**Cons:** requires an upfront brand-context setup, slightly steeper learning curve than a one-screen generator.

## 8. How to post a carousel on LinkedIn

The upload flow is the same regardless of how you created the PDF:

1. Click "Start a post" on the LinkedIn home feed.
2. Click the document icon (looks like a sheet of paper) inside the post composer. On some accounts it's behind the "+" menu under "Add a document."
3. Upload your PDF (under 100MB, up to 300 pages — though nobody swipes past 15).
4. Add a document title. This appears above slide 1 and acts like a subhead.
5. Write a caption: first line is the hook (LinkedIn truncates after about 2 lines on mobile), then 3–5 lines of value, then a CTA that mirrors slide N.
6. Add 3–5 relevant hashtags at the end of the caption.
7. Publish — or schedule for a midweek morning in your audience's timezone.

## 9. Best practices checklist

- **8–12 slides total** — 10 is the safest default.
- **4:5 portrait at 1080×1350 px.**
- **Slide 1 must stop the scroll on its own** — don't bury the hook.
- **Maximum 25 words per slide.**
- **One idea per slide, no exceptions.**
- **Add a page indicator ("1/10")** so viewers know there's more.
- **End with a single, specific CTA** — not a generic "thanks for reading."
- **Keep the file under 100MB.**
- **For the first hour after posting, reply to every comment** — early engagement compounds.
- **Cadence: 1–2 carousels per week** is sustainable and effective.

## 10. Common mistakes to avoid

The single most common cause of a flat carousel is a weak first slide — no hook, no curiosity gap, no contrast against the feed. Close behind is too much text on each slide; if a slide reads like a paragraph, nobody finishes it on a phone. Many decks also forget the CTA slide entirely, ending on a value point and throwing away the momentum the carousel just earned. The flip side of that mistake is treating the slides as the entire post and leaving the caption empty, which loses every viewer who never swipes at all.

Smaller execution misses compound the same way. Body type under 24pt becomes unreadable on mobile. Inconsistent fonts, colors, and layouts across slides quietly scream "amateur" and erode trust before the CTA. Skipping the page indicator hides the length of the deck so viewers bail early. And posting then disappearing — no replies to the first wave of comments — kills the algorithmic boost just as it begins to compound.

## Frequently asked questions

**How many slides should a LinkedIn carousel have?** Aim for 8–12 slides total, with 10 as the safest default. Fewer than 8 slides can feel insubstantial; more than 12 tests viewer patience unless the content is exceptionally compelling.

**What is the best size for LinkedIn carousel slides?** The optimal size is 1080×1350 pixels (4:5 portrait ratio). This claims maximum vertical real estate in the LinkedIn feed and looks clean on mobile devices.

**Do LinkedIn carousels really get more engagement than text posts?** Yes. Industry benchmarks consistently show carousel engagement at roughly 3.7x text posts, with native document posts pulling about 6x more reach than posts that link out. They also see 3x higher save rates.

**Can I use AI to write LinkedIn carousels?** Yes — in two ways. Single-purpose carousel generators (PostNitro, AICarousels, and similar tools) take a topic or text and split it into slides. AI agents like Dynal own the full loop from brief to published post, including research, outline, copy, design, and scheduling.

**Can I edit a LinkedIn carousel after posting?** No. Once a carousel is posted, LinkedIn renders it as static slides — you cannot edit the PDF or the individual slides. Always preview thoroughly before publishing.

**How often should I post LinkedIn carousels?** One to two carousels per week is the sustainable sweet spot. It is enough to stay visible and build a content library without burning out your team or diluting quality with filler decks.
